# 02.2 · Open coding: writing notes on failures

Open coding is writing down what you see in a failed answer, in your own words, before you give it a name. The discipline is small and it is the whole difference between an error analysis that finds things and one that confirms what you already believed.

## Why it matters

A team has a list of failure categories from a meeting six weeks ago: wrong answer, too slow, wrong tone. They start labelling traces against it. It goes well, because labelling is fast — a trace with an error chip is `wrong answer`, a slow trace is `too slow`, everything else is `wrong tone`. Three weeks later they have a clean-looking table with three rows and a plan.

Here is what the plan got wrong. In this course's own data, the bot's tuned system produced 94 answers of which 47 failed. 27 of those 47 were **right but uncited** — the fact was correct and no source was named. Under the team's taxonomy those 27 are `wrong answer`, because `wrong answer` is the nearest box. So the team builds a judge for factual accuracy, ships it, and measures a pass rate that does not move: the answers were never factually wrong. Meanwhile the 6 refusals and the 2 invented policies are sitting inside the same `wrong answer` row, invisible, because nobody ever had a row for them.

The damage is not that they counted badly. It is that they cannot tell the difference between *the system failed this way* and *the system failed this way and we had no box for it*. Labelling first makes both look identical, because a label applied before reading is a filter: everything that does not fit is recorded as the nearest thing that does.

## How it works

An **open code** is one observation about one trace, written so that a stranger could check it. Three properties, all of them testable:

1. **It is an observation, not a conclusion.** "The answer is uncited" is checkable. "The bot hallucinates" is a label you have invented and then found evidence for.
2. **It names the evidence.** A note that says *which span*, *which number*, *which field* can be re-checked in a month against the same trace. A note that says "the answer was bad" cannot.
3. **Someone else reading the same trace would write a similar note.** This is the real test, and it is why "in my own words" is not a licence for private shorthand.

One note per trace, one observation per note. If you have two observations, write two notes — a note with two claims fails property 2, because half of it will be unverifiable by the time you look again.

Here are the two pairs that matter, taken from the default batch you are about to read. Both traces are real; both were generated by the lab's educational model.

**The fifth trace in your batch** — "When is a vendor re-reviewed?" — the user pressed thumbs-up, and the `citation check` span carries `error: answer contained no handbook citation`. The `chat` span shows 72 output tokens.

| Weak | Strong |
|---|---|
| `no_citation` | The user was satisfied and the check still failed it: no handbook section named. The answer is 72 output tokens, so it is short. The trace does not say whether the re-review rule itself is right. |

The weak version is a label — it is what you would have ticked anyway, and it records nothing you can re-check. The strong version contains two observations (the user's reaction and the check's verdict, which point in opposite directions) and one explicit limit (the rule's truth is not visible here). That limit is the most valuable word in the note, because it tells the next person where to look.

**The third trace in your batch** — "How many mandatory training days do I get?" — thumbs-down, and every field healthy: retrieval ran for 78 ms, the post-check reported no error, the answer is 90 output tokens.

| Weak | Strong |
|---|---|
| `hallucination` | The user pressed thumbs down and nothing in the pipeline complained. Retrieval ran, the check passed, the answer is 90 tokens. So the failure is in the content of the answer and no span in this trace can see it. |
| `bad quality, fix the prompt` | The user pressed thumbs down and nothing in the pipeline complained: retrieval ran 78 ms, the post-check reported no error, the answer is 90 output tokens. Whatever went wrong is invisible here. |

"Fix the prompt" in the note is the tell. A note that carries its own remedy is not an observation any more — it is a decision, made before you have two more examples of the same thing, and it will quietly shape what you see in the next ten traces.

## Show me

The lab's note box sits above the tag checkboxes on purpose, and its hint says it plainly: *write the observation before you pick a label; if you cannot write it, you have not understood the failure yet, and a tag would only hide that.* The scoreboard for this lesson is the **Progress** panel, which reports two numbers: traces read, and distinct failure modes named. The second number only moves when a tag appears, so it is a count of what you have managed to name — which is exactly why the notes underneath it are the real work.

What you can honestly expect to name from this batch is worth knowing before you start. The traces carry exactly one failure signal: the 27 citation-check errors, which is 27 of 94. The shipped labels for this bot say 47 of its 94 tuned answers failed. So **at least 20 of the failures in this batch cannot be named from the trace at all** — 12 wrong facts, 6 refusals and 2 invented policies all pass a clean post-check, and no span field distinguishes them from a good answer.

That gap is not a flaw in the exercise. It is the lesson's real finding: an open note records what you can see, and the limit of what you can see is part of the note. When a note ends in "and nothing here can tell me", you have just written down the strongest argument for going and reading the actual answers — which, in your own system, is one click away and which you should do on every trace you label.

## Try it

**Predict first.** Write down, before opening the lab: after ten traces, how many distinct failure modes do you expect to have named, and what are they? Then check your answer against the Progress panel when you get there. Most people predict two or three and a large `no_citation` row.

[Simulation: error-analysis](../../simulations/error-analysis/index.html)

1. **Note first, ten times.** For the first ten traces, write the note before you look at the checkboxes. Aim for one sentence per trace containing a span name or a number. Then tag, and notice how much faster the tag is than the note.
2. **Read trace 15 carefully.** "Does the company run a crèche or subsidise child care?" is one of only seven questions in the whole set about something the handbook is silent on. Write a note about what the *question* is, before you think about the answer.
3. **Find a trace the checkboxes cannot describe.** Open the "Write your own tag" panel and create a tag from your own observation — length is a good one, since `output_tokens` is right there in the span. Add it and watch it appear in the counts table.
4. **Try the swap test.** Pick the note you are proudest of, read it again with no memory of the trace, and check every claim in it against the span fields. Count the claims you cannot check.

## Break it

Tick `no_citation` on five traces before writing anything, then read those five with the box already ticked.

You will find that your notes collapse. Three of them become "another uncited answer", and the Progress panel reports **1 distinct failure mode after 5 traces**. You have learned nothing — but the panel reads exactly like success, because a taxonomy with one category always looks tidy.

The lie is this: *a label applied before reading is a filter that deletes evidence.* Everything you did not expect gets recorded as the nearest thing you did expect, the count stays clean, and the one thing you could have discovered is the one thing you can no longer see. Notice also that **Skip counts a trace as read**: both buttons advance and add the trace to your denominator, so skipping eight traces lowers every share in the counts table without lowering the trace count. A taxonomy built by skipping looks smaller and more confident than one built by reading.

## Fix it

Write the note, then choose the tag, in that order — every time, including for the traces you are sure about.

On the scoreboard this looks like a counts table with the same numbers in it and a completely different feel underneath. Reading properly, your tags will spread out (in a default batch, no single tag should hold more than about a third of the traces), and the **Export notes and tags** button produces a JSON file where every tag has a note attached to it. That file is the artefact: a count with its evidence, which is something you can hand to a colleague, re-read in three months, and disagree with precisely.

## How do I know it works?

**Dev run.** Read 20 traces in the default random batch, writing a note before every tag.

**Target:**

- 20 notes, none empty.
- At least 15 notes containing a span name or a number.
- At least one note where none of the seven suggested tags fitted, and you wrote your own.
- At least 5 traces where you can state what the trace does **not** tell you.

**Validation, once.** Export your notes and tags as JSON and re-read five of them against the trace they describe, with the note hidden until you have re-checked the span fields yourself. Target: no note makes a claim the span fields do not support. If one does, you have found the exact habit this lesson is about.

## Use / don't use

**Use it for:** the first hour of any eval project, before a single metric exists; incident review, where the note is the part that gets pasted into the ticket and is still readable a year later; and handing analysis to someone who was not in the room. Notes are the cheapest artefact in the whole course: no model calls, no key, no infrastructure. In production they live in whatever your annotation store is — a column in your eval set, a field on the trace, a `notes` key in your dataset — and they are what turns a pile of traces into a dataset someone else can work from.

**Don't use it for:** writing verdicts ("pass", "bad answer"), writing the fix ("tighten the prompt"), or writing more than one observation per note. All three feel productive and all three make the note uncheckable later. And do not do it during an outage: labelling under pressure is exactly how you end up with a three-row taxonomy and a confident plan built on it.

## Reflect

1. Find the weakest note you wrote today. Which of the three properties does it fail, and what would it take to rewrite it so a stranger could check it?
2. The lab hides the answer text on purpose, and you named far fewer failure modes than the system actually has. In your own application, where would you put the answer so that every trace you open shows it next to the spans?
