# 01.4 · A tour of the Eval Lab

Nine browser labs, no install and no API key, and one rule about what the numbers in them are. This
lesson is the map: what each page is for, which knob it turns, how to tell a page that will not run from a
page that ran badly, and what this build does and does not contain.

## Why it matters

Three failures, all of them ordinary, and all of them avoidable in ninety seconds.

**The blank page.** A learner opens a lesson, clicks the simulation link, and gets a white screen. The
lesson is markdown on a website; it always loads. The lab is a JavaScript page, and a JavaScript page can
fail — JavaScript disabled, an old browser without ES modules, a corporate proxy rewriting `.js`, a cached
copy of an old data file. The learner has no way to tell a broken browser from a broken course, so they
assume the course. Some quit here, in lesson one, and never come back.

**The borrowed number.** A learner reads `Llama 3.2 3B · Plain · 0 shots, TPR 86.4%, kappa 0.43` on a
scoreboard, screenshots it, and posts it in a work chat as "what we get with Llama 3.2 3B". It is not a
measurement of Llama 3.2 3B. The verdicts behind it were generated by a seeded model of judge behaviour,
and the page says so in its footer, where nobody reads. This is the most expensive kind of course
mistake: it is not a wrong number, it is a right number attached to the wrong label, and once it is in a
chat it is unfalsifiable.

**The missing half.** This course is planned as fifteen modules. The build you are reading contains
modules 1 to 10 — the browser-only fundamentals path. Modules 11 to 15 and the capstone are not in it.
A learner who finishes module 10 looking for the Python and OpenTelemetry material will find nothing, and
the sensible conclusion — that the course promised more than it has — is worth pre-empting in one
paragraph rather than discovering at the end.

All three failures share a shape: something that is true was separated from the context that makes it
true, and the separation is invisible until it costs you.

## How it works

### The anatomy of a lab page

Every page in this course is built from the same four parts, so once you know one you know all of them:

| Part | Where it is | What it is for |
| ---- | ----------- | ------------- |
| **Controls** | left column | the knobs. Judge, answer, items, sample size, strategy, repeats, seed |
| **Scoreboard** | left, under the controls | *Pin as A* freezes the current run; the diff against A is the only comparison the page can make honestly |
| **Results** | right column | tiles, the confusion table, the cost panel, the one-way held-out button |
| **Footer** | bottom of the page | the educational-model banner. Read it once, here, and you will never quote a lab number wrongly |

The tiles are the three numbers from lesson 1.1, in a fixed order everywhere: **pass rate** (with its
interval), **agreement with humans** (kappa, TPR, TNR, accuracy), and, in the panels that need it, the
decision-specific extra. Modules 6, 7 and 10 add the rest.

The reason the scoreboard exists in this exact form is worth a sentence, because it is the whole of
lesson 1.3 in one interface: an unpinned number has no baseline, and a number without a baseline cannot be
diffed. The page is a snapshot. The scoreboard is the diff.

### The device check

Before any of that, the course asks one question: can this browser run the labs? The check runs six
checks, and the interesting design decision is that **two of them are marked optional and cannot fail the
page**:

| Check | What it proves | If it fails |
| ----- | -------------- | ----------- |
| ES modules | the lab loaded at all | the lab cannot run |
| WebAssembly | the statistics engine can validate a module | the lab cannot run |
| Local storage | pinned runs and notes survive a reload | the labs still work; nothing is kept between visits |
| Viewport ≥ 360px | the layout has room | the page is hard to read |
| Reduced motion | honoured if the OS asks for it | animations are on |
| WebGPU | a local model could run here | irrelevant: no lab in modules 1–10 needs it |

This is lesson 1.2's rule applied to the course's own tooling. "Will this browser work?" is not decidable;
"will it work *for these labs*?" is, and the honest answer needs the optional/broken distinction the page
prints in its verdict column. A check that reports one undifferentiated failure percentage would be
useless in exactly the way a vague success criterion is useless.

The page also times 100,000 resamples and buckets the result: under **120 ms** is comfortable, under
**400 ms** is usable, above that the knobs will feel sticky and you may want a smaller sample while
experimenting. That is the performance target from the course plan, stated as something you measure on
your own machine rather than as a promise.

### What "educational model" means, mechanically

The labs do not call a model. Each one ships a data file of **recorded per-item verdicts** — for every
item, every judge configuration, one verdict, plus up to five repeats — and a seeded generator. Your
browser recomputes the statistics from those verdicts every time you turn a knob. Same seed, same items,
same numbers, every time.

That design buys three things: the labs are free and instant, the arithmetic is genuinely real rather than
a screenshot, and a recorded verdict is only ever displayed for the exact configuration that produced it.
The third one is the integrity rule you saw in the prompt panel of lesson 1.2: type one character into the
judge prompt and the page refuses to show you another prompt's verdicts under the new prompt's name.

Swapping in genuine recordings of a real judge replaces **data files, not code**. That is the design
constraint, and it is why the honest statement can be made once, up front, and kept true for every lesson:
the arithmetic is real, the judge is a stand-in.

## Show me

The device check runs six checks and one benchmark, and reports a verdict per row rather than one
pass/fail: *works*, *not needed here*, or *will not work*. Two of the six — local storage and WebGPU —
are optional by design, because the labs still run without them.

The tour page is the smallest complete example of the whole course: a loop description on the left, and one
live computation on the right. It loads twenty items with human labels and a deliberately generous judge,
and shows **judge pass rate 75.0% (15 of 20)**, **kappa −0.12 (−0.44–0.30)**, **TPR 71.4%**, **TNR 16.7%**,
with the confusion table underneath and a callout telling you that four in five real problems sail through.
That is lesson 1.1 in one screen, computed live rather than printed.

This build contains these labs and no others:

| Lab | Module | What it is for |
| --- | ------ | -------------- |
| device-check | — | whether this browser can run the labs |
| tour | 1 | the loop, and what a judge score actually means |
| error-analysis | 2 | read traces, name failures in your own words, then count them, before any metric exists |
| code-checks | 3 | assertions and reference metrics, running live, with the code readable |
| eval-set | 4 | how many items, which ones, and where the line between dev and test goes |
| judge-lab | 5, 6, 7 | judge design, validation against human labels, biases, size and cost |
| rag-evals | 8 | grounding and abstention, the two failures retrieval metrics cannot see |
| agent-evals | 9 | the right answer reached by the wrong route, and what that costs |
| ab-gates | 10 | paired differences, the items that flipped, and what a release gate costs in false alarms |

**And here is what this build does not contain.** Modules 11 to 15 — the production path in Python and
OpenTelemetry, regression suites in CI, online evals and monitoring, and the advanced topics — plus the
capstone are planned and not built here. Modules 1 to 10 need nothing but a browser and are complete on
their own terms. If you were promised a Docker-and-`pytest` course, that is a later build, and nothing in
these ten modules depends on it.

## Try it

**Predict first.** Before you click anything: do you expect the device check to pass on your machine, and
do you expect the tour page's kappa to be positive or negative? Write both down. The first is a guess about
your setup, the second is a guess you can check in ten seconds.

[Simulation: tour](../../simulations/tour/index.html)

1. **Run the check.** Open the device check. Read all six rows, not just the verdict at the top, because
   the two optional rows are the interesting ones. Then press **Run benchmark** and write down your
   milliseconds. If it is above 400, use that number for the rest of the course: sample sizes around 40
   instead of 57, and expect the sliders to lag a frame.
2. **Read the "what this page did not do" panel.** It says the page made no network requests, on purpose,
   because a device check that phones home is not a check. Notice that the whole fundamentals path has the
   same property: nothing you type, including a judge prompt you edit, leaves your machine. That is worth
   knowing before you paste a real customer question into a lab.
3. **Open the tour and count before you read.** Cover the tiles. Read the confusion table, compute the
   pass rate, the TPR and the TNR yourself, then look. Same exercise as lesson 1.1 — do it anyway, because
   the habit of computing before reading is the habit the whole course is trying to build.
4. **Find the honest footer.** Every lab has one. Read it on the tour page and on the judge lab, and be
   able to say from memory what it does and does not license you to claim.

## Break it

The easiest way to break these labs is the oldest one: turn the engine off.

- **JavaScript disabled.** The lesson loads; the lab is a blank page. The `<noscript>` fallback says
  "This simulation needs JavaScript" and no more. Nothing tells the learner that the markdown is fine and
  the lab is not.
- **Local storage blocked.** Common inside a sandboxed frame or a locked-down corporate browser. The
  device check reports it as optional and says the labs still work and nothing is kept between visits —
  which is true, and which means your pinned run A is gone when you reload, mid-experiment.
- **A narrow viewport.** Under 360px the layout stops being readable, and a misread tile is worse than no
  tile.
- **An old cached data file.** The newest lab code asks for a column the cached data file does not have,
  and the page throws instead of rendering. This is the failure the device check cannot catch, and the
  reason its verdict column exists: "it ran" and "it ran correctly" are different claims.

And the break that costs the most, which is not technical at all: **read the judge name as the subject of
the number.** `Llama 3.2 3B · Plain · 0 shots — kappa 0.43, TPR 86.4%` is a measurement of *a recorded
run*, and the model name is the label of the run. Turn that sentence into "Llama 3.2 3B has kappa 0.43
with humans" and you have published a benchmark that does not exist. The name is on the page because the
recording was made against that model configuration; the number is on the page because the recording is
what the lab replays.

## Fix it

**For the browser.** The device check names the repair itself: a current Chrome, Edge, Firefox or Safari,
with JavaScript enabled for the site, and if storage is blocked, accept that pins do not survive a reload
and finish each experiment in one sitting. Re-run the check on a second device if something looks wrong
in a lab — a phone will usually tell you whether the problem is your machine or the page. If a lab still
fails after the check passes, it is a bug in the course, and the footer is where to say so.

**For the number.** The rule, in one line, and it applies to every lab in every module:

> A number from a lab is a measurement of a **recorded run**, and a recorded run is named by the exact
> configuration that produced it — the judge prompt, the model, the items, the seed, the version of the
> data file. Never quote one without that name, and never quote one as a property of a model in the wild.

Said positively: the labs are honest about their own instruments, which is why you can trust the
arithmetic, reason about the statistics, and build the habits this course is for. A course that hid the
substitution would let you learn the arithmetic and leave you with a habit of quoting numbers you cannot
defend. The banner is there so that "this is a modelled judge" is part of every sentence you say about a
lab number, rather than a footnote you read once and forget.

**For the missing half.** Nothing to fix; it is a fact about this build, stated above. The right response
is to plan against what exists: modules 1 to 10 are a complete course in evaluating and observing an LLM
application, and the production path is a separate follow-on that assumes you have finished them.

## How do I know it works?

**Challenge, without opening a single lesson.** Do these three things in order:

1. **Name every lab and its knob, from memory.** Nine labs: which module, which knob does it turn, and
   what single number would move if you turned it? If you can do that, you understand the shape of the
   whole course and every later lesson is a detail on a structure you already have.
2. **State the three numbers every lab shows** — pass rate, agreement with humans, interval width — and
   the one control that makes the last one narrower. Then say which of the three is a statement about the
   system and which is a statement about the check. If the last part takes more than a second, go back to
   lesson 1.1.
3. **State the honesty rule in your own words**, including the thing you may never do with a lab number. If
   you can do that without looking, you are ready for module 2, which starts reading traces.

A fourth, optional check for the curious: open the judge lab, set *Items* to **Test (37)**, and read the
verdict. It tells you that test agreement is only revealed by pressing Validate. That one-way button is
the smallest possible version of the discipline this course is about, and noticing it unprompted is a good
sign.

## Use / don't use

**Do use** the labs for the thing they are good at: understanding a mechanism fast enough that you can
predict a number before you see it. Nothing else in your week will let you turn a judge prompt, watch the
TNR move, and understand why in under a minute. That loop speed is the entire pedagogical argument for
these pages.

**Don't use** them to settle an argument about a real model, a real vendor or a real benchmark. The
recordings are modelled; the arithmetic is real; the combination is a teaching instrument and not a
measurement. If you need a real number, the production path in a later build is where your own judge runs
and your own human labels go — and the shape of what you do there is exactly what these labs rehearse.

**Don't use** the course as a substitute for reading your own outputs. Module 2 exists because step one of
the loop is reading, and a lab is a fixed corpus. The moment you start an eval for a real feature, the
first thing you must do is sit with twenty of its real answers, and no lab on this list can do that for
you. Labs teach the instrument; your own traces are the measurement.

In production, the shape is the same and the cost model is not. Everything in modules 1 to 10 is free
because the expensive part — calling a judge on every item — was done before you arrived. In production
that call is per item, per run, per CI job; the cost panel on the judge lab exists so you can see the
arithmetic of it before you are the one paying. A 40-item run is $1.52 at the modelled price, which is
nothing; 5,000 items a night, five repeats, every night, is a line item, and the size of that line item is
the reason module 4 spends a whole module on choosing how many items you need.

2. Pick a feature you own. Name the one lab from the table above that would be most useful for it, the
